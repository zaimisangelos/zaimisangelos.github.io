Review before release,recourse after?

An analysis of AI accountability routes in Japanese technology firms

Review before an AI system is released, and a route to recourse afterwards: a way to raise a concern and ask for it to be looked at again and put right. How far do 18 Japanese technology firms describe each of the two in their public documents? This study compared 142 official sources.

This page summarises the study shown on the poster at the venue.

1

Background

Suppose an automated AI decision suddenly cuts you off from a service. Where do you raise it? Who looks at it again? Will you get an answer? If it was a mistake, will it be corrected?

Most large technology firms publish principles for the AI they build.

But for a person disadvantaged by an AI system in use, a principle is worth what it leads to (Bovens 2007; Cobbe et al. 2021). Four things are needed:

  1. 1Somewhere to raise the concern
  2. 2Someone with authority to review it
  3. 3A response
  4. 4A correction, where warranted

AI governance is said to invest in review before deployment and much less in recourse afterwards (Pi & Proctor 2025). How firms document each of the two has hardly been examined.

2

Questions

  1. Within the same firm, is review before release documented more fully than recourse after release?
  2. Do public documents state that concerns raised from outside reach the body that governs the firm’s AI?
  3. Which routes are open to outsiders, and how are they linked to AI?
  4. Does the Hiroshima AI Process (HAIP) questionnaire ask about recourse as well as review?

Five expectations, and the rules for judging them, were fixed in a dated plan before the main coding of the documents began. The results are in “6 Expectations and results”.

3

Data and method

Using the documents firms publish, the study compared review and recourse routes element by element, in pairs.

18 Japanese technology firms9 that published a HAIP report in 2025, and 9 comparison firms
142 official sources from all 18 firms133 company web pages and reports, and 9 HAIP reports
Review and every route coded on paired elementsEach element classed as stated, partly stated or not located
743 coding decisionsEach recorded with the passage it rests on, or the sources searched

HAIP reports are not part of the main comparison. The set of sources was closed on 22 October 2026.

Three codes

  • StatedThe element is clearly written in the documents.

  • Partly statedOnly part of it is stated, the wording is vague, or it is stated for only part of what it applies to.

  • Not locatedNot found in the sources examined.

“Not located” does not mean that the mechanism does not exist. It means that it was not found in the public documents.

5

Conclusions

ReviewWritten in AI governance documents
The connection is rarely stated
RecourseWritten in privacy, compliance and human-rights routes
  • Review and recourse are written in different places.
  • What firms rarely state is the connection between the two: whether a concern about an AI decision reaches someone who can change the system.
  • Suggestions (their effect has not been tested): firms can state this connection for each route. Frameworks such as HAIP can ask about recourse alongside review.

7

Limitations

  • The study covers only what firms publish. An element coded “not located” may exist inside the firm.
  • All coding followed one procedure and one codebook, and re-readings were recorded. It was not checked by an independent second coder.
  • The 18 firms were chosen purposively, and most are active in disclosure. The results cannot be generalised to Japanese industry as a whole.

Common questions

Does “not located” mean the firm does not have that element?

Not necessarily. The study examined only the documents firms publish. An element that was not found there may exist inside the firm.

Is the conclusion that firms have no recourse mechanisms?

No. 15 firms had a route with at least three elements stated, and in every one of them the most complete route was an established mechanism, such as a privacy or compliance procedure. What was rarely stated is the connection between those routes and AI governance.

Do the results apply to Japanese companies in general?

That cannot be said. The 18 firms were chosen purposively, and most are active in disclosure. The results cannot be generalised to Japanese industry as a whole.

Glossary

AI governanceAIガバナンス
The policies, structures and procedures for developing, providing and using AI appropriately.
Accountabilityアカウンタビリティ
The responsibility to explain one’s decisions and actions, to answer questions about them and to put things right where needed.
Review審査
The internal procedure, before an AI system is released, for checking risks and deciding whether it may go ahead.
Recourse救済
The channels and procedures through which a person with a concern about an AI decision or behaviour can raise it and ask for review, a response and correction. On this page it means that whole process, not only compensation.
Route, channel経路・窓口
A contact point or procedure for raising a concern or complaint with a firm. Examples are privacy (personal data) procedures, compliance hotlines and human-rights hotlines.
AI-linked routeAIに結びついた経路
A route specific to AI, or one that the firm’s documents explicitly connect to AI.
Most complete route最も充実した経路
Among the routes compared in each analysis, the one with the most of the six elements stated. It is not a rating of how well concerns are handled or how easy a remedy is to obtain.
Escalationエスカレーション
Passing a case from the first handler to a more senior person or body, or to an outside body.
Codingコーディング
A method of analysis: reading documents and classifying their content against criteria set in advance.
Sensitivity analysis感度分析
An analysis that checks whether the conclusion holds when the counting rules or the conditions are changed.
Hiroshima AI Process (HAIP)広島AIプロセス
An international framework for rules on AI, launched in 2023 at the G7 under Japan’s presidency. Under its reporting framework, organisations that develop AI report voluntarily on how they follow the International Code of Conduct; it has been run on the OECD’s website since February 2025 (press release from Japan’s Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, in Japanese).

Report a problem with the AI chatbot

This study asked whether a concern about AI reaches someone with authority and leads to a response and a correction. The same question applies to the AI chatbot on this page. So its reporting route is set out here, using the elements from the study.

  • Provided
  • Not provided

Entry

Anyone who has used this chatbot can report a problem. Reports are also accepted from companies or organisations mentioned in an answer.

Write to angeloszaimis@keio.jp with the subject “AI chatbot report”.

Reviewer with authority

Reports are read by the author himself (Angelos Zaimis). The author has the authority to change the chatbot’s instructions and source documents, and to switch the chatbot off.

Information for the person

The person who reports is told which passage of which document the answer was based on, and what the author checked.

Response to the person

The author replies by email within 7 days of receiving the report. The reply says what was corrected or, if nothing was corrected, why not.

Escalation

If you are not satisfied with how the author handled the report, there is at present no procedure for someone other than the author to look at it again.

Systemic feedback

Reports are used to revise the chatbot’s instructions and source documents, so that the same mistake is not repeated.

Individual remedy

If a mistake is confirmed, the chatbot is corrected and the person who reported is told what was corrected. If a correction cannot prevent the problem, the chatbot is switched off.

What happens after a report

The author runs this chatbot alone. When a report arrives, he takes these steps in order.

  1. ReceiptThe date and the content of the report are recorded.
  2. Emergency stopIf the answer could harm a company or a person, the chatbot is switched off first, until the check is finished.
  3. Checking the factsIf the chat record still exists, the question and the answer are located. The answer is then compared with the relevant passage of the paper.
  4. DecisionThe author decides whether the answer departs from the paper, adds something the paper does not say, or describes a company or a person inaccurately or unfairly. If no problem is found, the reason is written down.
  5. ActionIf there was a problem, the chatbot’s instructions or source documents are corrected, and the same question is asked again to confirm the fix. If a correction cannot prevent the problem, the chatbot is switched off.
  6. ReplyThe person who reported is told by email, within 7 days of receipt, what was checked, what was decided and what was done.
  7. RecordThe report, the decision, the action and the dates are kept on record.

What to include in a report

  1. What you asked
  2. The AI’s answer (as it was, if possible)
  3. The date and time of use
  4. What you think is wrong
  5. Whether you would like a reply
